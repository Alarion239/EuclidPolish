 \block{Model Architecture}{
    We use the POLISH/POLISH++ software (Connor+2022, Wu+2026), built on the
\emph{Wide Activation for Efficient and Accurate Image Super-Resolution}
(WDSR-B; Yu+2018) architecture --- a deep convolutional neural network (CNN).
The model stacks \textbf{32} wide-activation residual blocks (each expanding
the internal feature channels by $\times6$), \textbf{99} weight-normalised
convolutions, a global residual skip connection, and a $\times2$ pixel-shuffle
upsampling stage, for \textbf{599{,}328 trainable parameters}. It takes all
four available Euclid filters as input --- VIS plus the three near-infrared
bands Y/J/H, each sampled at $0.10''$/pix --- and returns a single deconvolved
VIS image super-resolved to $0.05''$/pix. Because the sky spans a huge range of
brightnesses, we first apply an \emph{asinh} stretch to compress the dynamic
range, then train against an $L_1$ loss (the mean absolute difference between
the predicted and reference images).
  }

  \block{Summary}{
    Strong gravitational lensing yields systems that are valuable because they allow for studies of distant magnified galaxies that would otherwise be too faint to see, the mass distribution of the foreground galaxy or cluster, dark matter, and even the geometry of the Universe. 
    In particular, lenses with \emph{small Einstein radii} (the angular distance between the lens and the source) are especially valuable, since they constrain the dark-matter halo profile. 
    However, when this radius is too small, the lens cannot be visually separated from the source in low spatial resolution, wide-area surveys, which causes underrepresentation of lenses with small Einstein radii in studies. 
    Recent advances in super-resolution techniques offer a way to recover this missing population by improving the identification of strong gravitational lenses in Euclid surveys, the first wide-area space-based optical survey. 
    Building on POLISH \citep{Connor2022} and POLISH++ \citep{Wu2026}, originally developed for radio astronomy, we increase the resolution of Euclid images to the level of higher spatial resolution Hubble images. 
    We train on synthetic Euclid-like images built from simulated galaxies blurred with the extracted Euclid point spread function, and benchmarked against Hubble images. 
    Our preliminary results show that, applied to real Euclid data, the model both super-resolves \emph{and} denoises, revealing structure that is blurred or buried in the originals --- a promising first step toward recovering the missing small-radius lenses.
  }
